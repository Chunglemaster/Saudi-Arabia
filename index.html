Saudi Arabia: Where Earth Shapes a Kingdom

Saudi Arabia is a country in Western Asia that covers about 80 percent of the Arabian Peninsula, roughly 2.15 million square kilometers. Its capital is Riyadh. The modern kingdom was unified in 1932 by King Abdulaziz Ibn Saud, but Arabian history stretches back thousands of years. Caravan cities along the Incense Route linked Yemen to the Mediterranean, and Mecca and Medina became the holiest cities in Islam, drawing millions of pilgrims every year for Hajj and Umrah.

Culturally, the country is shaped by Islam, Arabic language, desert hospitality traditions, and Bedouin heritage. Economically, Saudi Arabia is one of the world's largest oil producers, and its national company, Saudi Aramco, is among the largest energy companies in the world. Through Vision 2030, the government is working to diversify into mining, tourism, renewable energy, and technology. This website explores how Earth's internal and external processes (plate tectonics, volcanoes, wind, water, and climate) have shaped Saudi Arabia's land, people, and economy.

An Interesting Fact About Saudi Arabia's Physical Geography

Saudi Arabia has no permanent rivers or natural lakes, yet it contains the Rub' al Khali, or Empty Quarter, the largest continuous sand desert in the world at about 650,000 square kilometers. Its water comes mostly from ancient groundwater, rare flash floods in dry riverbeds called wadis, and desalination.

Questions and Answers

On which continent and in what general area is Saudi Arabia located?

Saudi Arabia is in Asia, in the southwestern region known as Western Asia or the Middle East. It occupies most of the Arabian Peninsula.

What is the latitudinal range?

Saudi Arabia stretches from about 16°N in the south to about 32°N in the north, a range of roughly 16 degrees of latitude. It lies mostly in the subtropical desert zone.

During what time periods has it existed?

The First Saudi State lasted from 1727 to 1818, the Second Saudi State from 1824 to 1891, and the modern Kingdom of Saudi Arabia was declared on September 23, 1932, after Riyadh was recaptured in 1902. The region itself has been inhabited for thousands of years.

Famous cartographers

Muhammad al-Idrisi (1100-1165) was an Arab geographer born in Ceuta who worked for King Roger II of Sicily. His 1154 Tabula Rogeriana was the most accurate world map for centuries and included the Arabian Peninsula.

Al-Hamdani (10th century) was a geographer from Yemen whose book Sifat Jazirat al-Arab described the Arabian Peninsula in detail.

Carsten Niebuhr (1733-1815) was a Danish cartographer who joined an expedition to Arabia in 1761-1767; his maps and 1772 book were used in Europe for decades.

Plate Tectonics, Earthquakes & Volcanoes

Tectonic Plates and Natural Disasters

Saudi Arabia sits mostly on the Arabian Plate. Along its western edge, the Red Sea is a divergent boundary where the Arabian Plate is pulling away from the African (Nubian) Plate. To the northwest, the Gulf of Aqaba is part of the Dead Sea Transform, a transform boundary. To the northeast and east, the Arabian Plate collides with the Eurasian Plate in the Zagros Mountains, a convergent boundary.

Most of the country sits in the stable interior, so large earthquakes are uncommon, but earthquakes, volcanic activity, and flash floods do occur. Tsunami risk is low.

Rock and Mineral Resources

The western Arabian Shield contains very old igneous and metamorphic rocks rich in gold, copper, zinc, silver, and phosphate, while the eastern Arabian Platform is covered by sedimentary rocks such as limestone and sandstone that trap oil and natural gas. The country also has bauxite, phosphate, limestone, gypsum, and silica sand. The national mining company, Ma'aden, runs major operations for gold, phosphate, and aluminum.

Crude oil and petrochemicals make up most exports, and major buyers include China, India, Japan, South Korea, and the United States. Phosphate fertilizers and metals add to export income. Mining and oil provide many jobs, and Vision 2030 aims to expand mining as a second major industry.

Environmental impacts include greenhouse gas emissions, flaring, and heavy water use, and economic dependence on oil makes the country vulnerable to price swings.

Earthquakes

Saudi Arabia experiences small to moderate earthquakes, mainly near the Red Sea and Gulf of Aqaba. The 1995 Gulf of Aqaba earthquake (magnitude about 7.2) was felt across the region and damaged areas in northwest Saudi Arabia. In 2009, thousands of small earthquakes near Harrat Lunayyir caused by rising magma led to the evacuation of nearby villages.

The Saudi Geological Survey operates a national seismic network to monitor activity, and the Saudi Building Code sets seismic design rules. The Civil Defense authority issues warnings and educates the public.

Mountain Ranges

The Sarawat Mountains run along the Red Sea coast and are divided into the Hejaz in the north and the Asir in the south. Jabal Sawda, near Abha, reaches about 3,000 meters and is the highest point in the country. The Tuwaiq Escarpment is a curved limestone cliff about 800 kilometers long in central Arabia. The Aja and Salma mountains stand near Ha'il.

These mountains catch moisture and give the Asir region far more rain than the rest of the country, supporting terraced farming and cooler weather. Mountain passes guided Hajj and caravan routes, and cliffs and highlands historically gave protection to settlements and forts.

Volcanoes

Saudi Arabia has no tall composite volcanoes but has some of the largest lava fields in the world, called harrats, including Harrat Rahat, Harrat Khaybar, Harrat Kishb, Harrat Lunayyir, and Harrat Uwayrid. They are made mostly of basalt, with many small cinder (scoria) cones and some maar craters such as Al Wahbah crater (about 2 kilometers wide).

Historical eruptions include one near Khaybar around 640 CE and the 1256 Harrat Rahat eruption near Medina, when lava flowed toward the city but stopped before reaching it. Volcanic soil supports farming, craters attract tourism, and the lava fields could pose a future threat to nearby towns, so they are monitored.

Weathering, Mass Wasting & Erosion

Weathering and Mass Wasting

Saudi Arabia's dry climate favors physical weathering. Large day-to-night temperature swings crack rock, and salt crystals wedge rocks apart. Chemical weathering is slower but occurs where there is moisture: carbonation dissolves limestone to form caves and sinkholes, and hydrolysis breaks down feldspar in granite in the wetter Asir mountains.

Mass wasting such as rockfalls and landslides occurs on steep slopes after heavy rains, especially in the Hejaz and Asir. Authorities reduce risk with slope stabilization, retaining walls, and rock netting on mountain roads such as the Taif-Al Hada road, and the Saudi Geological Survey studies geologic hazards.

Water Erosion

Saudi Arabia has no permanent rivers, but wadis (dry riverbeds) carry floodwater during rare storms. Important wadis include Wadi ar-Rummah (one of the longest, over 600 km, flowing across central Arabia), Wadi Hanifa near Riyadh, Wadi Bisha, Wadi Najran, and Wadi Jizan. In wide valleys wadis form braided channels, while narrow bedrock canyons are straighter; true meanders are rare.

Flash floods erode channels, wash out roads, and damage towns. The government has built hundreds of dams to control flooding and recharge groundwater, and restored Wadi Hanifa as a green corridor in Riyadh.

Wind Erosion

Wind erosion is a major process. Strong shamal winds and dust storms move sand and dust across the country, scouring rocks into yardangs and building dunes that can cover roads and farms. Mitigation includes planting windbreaks and stabilizing dunes with vegetation and fences, and the Saudi Green Initiative aims to plant 10 billion trees and expand vegetation cover.

Deserts and Desertification

Deserts cover most of the country. The Rub' al Khali (Empty Quarter) is the largest sand sea, or erg. An-Nafud in the north is a red erg with crescent dunes. Ad-Dahna is a narrow band of red sand dunes (erg) connecting the two. The Hamad, along the north, is a stony hamada plateau, and gravel plains (reg) cover areas like the Summan Plateau.

Desertification is a growing problem due to overgrazing, groundwater depletion, and urban expansion. Saudi Arabia hosted the UN desertification conference (UNCCD COP16) in Riyadh in 2024.

Glaciers

There are no glaciers in Saudi Arabia today. However, rocks in the Sarah Formation preserve evidence of a major glaciation about 445 million years ago, when Arabia was part of the supercontinent Gondwana near the South Pole, leaving glacial deposits and scratch marks.

When those glaciers melted, organic-rich shales formed and became important oil and gas source rocks, so ancient ice indirectly helped create the country's oil wealth.

Fluvial Processes, Oceans & Coastlines

Flooding

Flooding is a real hazard even in the desert. Rare, intense storms cause flash floods in wadis and cities such as Jeddah, where floods in November 2009 killed more than 100 people and again in 2011. Other floods hit Riyadh, Najran, and Asir. The government has built dams, drainage networks, and flood channels, upgraded early-warning systems through the National Center for Meteorology, and Civil Defense issues rain alerts.

Land and River Trade Routes

Historically, the Incense Route and Hajj routes such as Darb Zubaydah connected Arabia with Mesopotamia and the Mediterranean, following wadis and oases because geology controls where water and passable terrain exist. Today, highways, the East-West oil pipeline, the Haramain high-speed railway between Mecca and Medina via Jeddah, and the King Fahd Causeway to Bahrain carry goods and people.

Advantages include access to oases and holy cities; disadvantages are long desert distances, sand dunes, escarpments, and flash floods. Main trading partners include China, India, Japan, South Korea, the United States, and the UAE. In 2025, China received 14.6 percent of Saudi merchandise exports, followed by the UAE at 10 percent and India at 9.4 percent, and China supplied 27.5 percent of Saudi imports, followed by the United States at 8.2 percent and the UAE at 5.7 percent, according to the General Authority for Statistics (Arab News, 2026).

Oceans, Ocean Trade Routes & Islands

The Red Sea and Persian Gulf are Saudi Arabia's two seas, linking to the Indian Ocean through Bab al-Mandab and the Strait of Hormuz. Currents in the Persian Gulf circulate counterclockwise, and Red Sea water exchanges with the Gulf of Aden; both seas are warm, salty, and humid, so coastal areas are far more humid than the interior. Seasonal monsoon winds bring moisture to the southwest.

Historically, ships used the Red Sea for trade between India, East Africa, and the Mediterranean, and today oil leaves from Ras Tanura and ships move through Jeddah Islamic Port, King Abdulaziz Port in Dammam, and Jubail, with the Suez Canal linking to Europe. The country is not landlocked.

Islands include the coral-rich Farasan Islands in the Red Sea, protected as a marine sanctuary, and Tarut Island in the Persian Gulf, one of the oldest settled places in the region. They support fishing, tourism, and heritage.

Coastlines

The Red Sea coast, about 1,800 km long, has a narrow coastal plain (the Tihamah), coral reefs, and rocky inlets called sharms, making it fairly irregular. The Persian Gulf coast, about 560 km long, is low, flat, and sandy with salt flats (sabkhas) and shallow bays.

Coral reefs support tourism and fishing and protect shores, while shallow Gulf waters allow ports and desalination plants but make coasts vulnerable to rising seas and pollution.

Hurricanes and Tsunamis

Saudi Arabia is rarely hit by hurricanes or cyclones because it is far from the Arabian Sea storm track; Cyclone Gonu (2007) and Cyclone Shaheen (2021) hit nearby Oman. Tsunami risk is low, though a historic earthquake in the Gulf of Aqaba in 1068 and the 1995 Aqaba earthquake produced small tsunamis along that coast.
